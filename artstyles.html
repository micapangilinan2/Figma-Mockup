VARIOUS ART STYLES THAT HAVE DEVELOPED IN YEARS

← Swipe left

Abstract Expressionism

  • Emerged back in the 1940-1950's.
  • It is expressed through expressive brush strokes or large, flat areas of single color.
  • Abstract Expressionism can also be used to express emotions through an artist's brush stroke.

Examples:

  • Wassily Kandinsky, Composition X, 1939. Courtesy of wassilykandinsky.net
  • Helen Frankenthaler, Mountains and Sea, 1952. Courtesy of Guggenheim Bilbao
  • Amy Donaldson, Breakthrough. Courtesy of artworkarchive.com

Geometric Art

  • The ancient Greek Period formally began around 900 BC, whereas Geometric Abstraction emerged in the year 1915.
  • This artform are created through points, lines, angles, and different shapes.
  • Shapes that are used may sometimes require mathematical precision.

Examples:

  • Josef Albers, Homage to the Square, 1969. Courtesy of Guggenheim New York
  • Josef Albers, Memento, 1943. Courtesy of Guggenheim New York
  • Sarah Morris, Midtown Paintings, 1998-2001. Courtesy of Petzel Gallery

Figurative Art

  • Figurative Art has existed way back in the Paleolithic era, and has evolved since to this day.
  • The 14th-17th century, specifically the Renaissance represents the peak of art in the history of figurative art.
  • This art style is focused on humans, animals, and objects that are actual sources.

Examples:

  • Pablo Picasso, The Three Dancers, 1925. Courtesy of Tate
  • Claude Monet, Woman with a Parasol - Madame Monet and Her Son, 1875. Courtesy of claude-monet.com
  • Lucian Freud, Standing by the Rags, 1988-8. Courtesy of Tate

Minimalist Art

  • Originated back in the late 1950-1960’s.
  • Minimalist arts are usually emphasized that art should only refer to itself.
  • It is focused purely on its physical components. Frank Stella had emphasized this by saying “What you see is what you see”.

Examples:

  • Robert Morris, Untitled (Mirrored Cube), 1965. Courtesy of Perez Art Museum Miami
  • Dan Flavin, “monument” for V. Tatlin, 1964. Courtesy of Tate
  • Tony Smith, Free Ride, 1962-1968. Courtesy of Saint Louis Art Museum

Pop Art

  • Reached its peak in the year 1960.
  • Artists used imagery from advertisements, comic books, product packaging, and media to create art.

Examples:

  • Richard Hamilton, Just what is it that makes today's homes so different, so appealing?, 1956. Courtesy of Wikipedia
  • Deborah Kass, Jewish Jackie, 1992. Courtesy of MoMA
  • James Rosenquist, F-111, 1964-65. Courtesy of MoMA

Surrealism

  • Began in 1920’s.
  • Often interpreted dreams and memories through imagery, creating alternate realities that defined logical thought.

Examples:

  • Rene Magritte, The Treachery of Images, 1929. Courtesy of renemagritte.org
  • Andre Masson, Automatic Drawing, 1924. Courtesy of MoMA
  • Dorothea Tanning,The Girl, 1950. Courtesy of Tate

Impressionism

  • Was a 19th century art movement.
  • This art style originated on an exhibition in France, which featured famous artists.
  • Their artworks, with the implication of being “unfinished” coined the term of Impressionism.

Examples:

  • Bato Dugarzhapov, Breakfast on a grass, Courtesy of The Linausaurus
  • Claude Monet, Impression, Sunrise, 1872. Courtesy of wikipedia.org
  • Edouard Manet, Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe (The Luncheon on the Grass), 1863. Courtesy of manet.org

Cubism

  • Emerged in the early 20th century in Paris.
  • Has three components: geometricity, simultaneity of multiple perspectives, and exploring a “fourth dimension”.
  • This art has become the starting point for many abstract styles.

Examples:

  • Pablo Picasso, Girl with a Mandolin, 1910. Courtesy of Wikipedia
  • Georges Braque, Mandora, 1909-10. Courtesy of Tate
  • Roger de la Fresnaye, Artillery, 1911. Courtesy of The Metropolitan Museum of Art

Text-based Art

  • Became popular in the 1960’s.
  • This type of art blends written words with visual designs to create meaning, often conveying messages with deep meanings.

Examples:

  • Robert Indiana, Decade: Autoportrait, 1969. Courtesy of Clarendon Fine Art
  • Barbara Kruger, Untitled (Your body is a battleground), 1989. Courtesy of National Gallery of Art
  • Tracey Emin, This is Another Place, 2007. Courtesy of Sotheby’s

Hyperrealism

  • Also called “superrealism”.
  • Hyperrealism shows artworks that are like high-resolution photography.
  • Artists make detailed works, that may also express emotions or narratives.

Examples:

  • Charles bell, Circus Act, 1994. Courtesy of Artsy
  • Nataliya Bagatskaya, Still life in hyperrealism "Just Tender Peaches...", 2021. Courtesy of The Artling
  • Douglas W Newton, Violet and Red, 2021. Courtesy of The Artling
